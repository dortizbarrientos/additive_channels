Additive channels

How can a curved fitness relationship have a close linear description, and when can that description predict selection?

Start with the parents and their offspring counts. Follow the calculation through the population's genetic variation, reproductive contributions and inheritance.

This guide is additional repository material, separate from the paper and its numbered supplementary files. Six connected lessons use the examples and saved results from the additive-channels repository. Change a quantity to see its effect, then open the derivation when you want to follow the algebra.

Fitness variation and reproductive contribution

To describe variation in log expected fitness, we weight breeding values by how common they are. To calculate the mean among contributing parents, we also weight them by expected reproductive output. Predicting offspring requires an inheritance model.

Notation used throughout the guide

G is breeding-value variance in one trait and a covariance matrix in several traits. The local gradient is b and the Hessian is H. Stabilising curvature is written Γ = −H, or γ = −H for one trait. The linear share Ag concerns a quadratic description of log expected output.

Use the Symbols button at any point for the remaining definitions. Matrix expressions preserve multiplication order; scalar expressions are labelled as one-trait calculations.

Lesson 1

Expected reproduction and offspring counts

Two plants can have the same breeding value for height and leave different numbers of offspring. We first need a fitness relationship that allows that difference.

Keep the parents with zero offspring

Consider these constructed observations. Each row contains four plants assigned the same breeding value, with groups at −1, 0 and 1 in arbitrary trait units. The numbers are counts of surviving offspring during one reproductive episode. They are constructed for this calculation. Later lessons use a separately specified quadratic curve; the saved estimation dataset is also separate.

The lower group has eight offspring from four parents, so its mean is two. Removing its zero count would change the denominator to three. We would then estimate reproduction among parents that reproduced, rather than among all parents in that group.

Predict the effect of removing a zero

If the lower group's zero count is removed, will the estimated mean increase, decrease or stay the same?

Why the estimated mean increases

The total remains eight, but the denominator falls from four to three. The estimate increases from 2 to 8/3 ≈ 2.667, a rise of one third. Its logarithm increases from about 0.693 to 0.981. The underlying observations have not improved; the calculation now excludes a parent that contributed no offspring.

Breeding value identifies the genetic predictor

A breeding value adds the average effects of an individual's alleles on a specified trait in a specified population. For height, it has units of height. Those average effects depend on the population used to define them. A breeding value is therefore a population-specific statistical quantity, not a complete description of the individual's genotype.

Plants with the same breeding value can differ in observed height, environmental conditions and reproductive output. We treat breeding values as known in these teaching examples. Estimating them from measurements requires a model, which we examine later.

Write Z for breeding value and W for reproductive output. The conditional mean

WA(z) = E[W | Z = z]

is expected output for a parent with breeding value z. The expectation averages over the other sources of variation in output that remain at that breeding value. Its meaning depends on the population, conditions and reproductive episode being considered. The four-plant means above illustrate estimates of such a relationship; they do not establish its population values.

Take the logarithm after averaging

We define log expected fitness as

ℓ(z) = ln WA(z), provided WA(z) > 0.

Subtracting two log values gives ℓ(z₂) − ℓ(z₁) = ln[WA(z₂)/WA(z₁)]. A difference of ln(2) therefore means that the second expected output is twice the first. Individual zero counts are compatible with a positive conditional mean. They remain in the average, and we never need their individual logarithms. If a conditional mean is genuinely zero, ℓ is not finite there. A finite sample containing only zeros also cannot supply a finite log of its sample mean.

The log of a mean differs from the mean of logs

The distinction persists when every count is positive. Take counts 2 and 8. Their mean is (2 + 8)/2 = 5, so the log of their mean is ln(5) ≈ 1.609.

Averaging their logs instead gives [ln(2) + ln(8)]/2 = ln(4) ≈ 1.386. Exponentiating recovers 4, their geometric mean, rather than 5, their arithmetic mean. The two operations describe different quantities.

The additive-channel calculation uses variation in ln E[W | Z]. It does not use E[ln W | Z], and it does not calculate a variance share for individual offspring counts.

Calculate the group means without dropping zeros
from math import log

groups = {"lower": [0, 1, 3, 4],
          "middle": [1, 2, 4, 5],
          "higher": [0, 0, 1, 3]}

for name, counts in groups.items():
    # Every parent enters the denominator, including zero counts.
    mean_output = sum(counts) / len(counts)
    # The logarithm is applied once, to the positive group mean.
    log_mean = log(mean_output) if mean_output > 0 else None
    print(name, mean_output, log_mean)

We can now ask how a line describes the relationship between breeding value and log expected output. The variation among individual counts remains a separate source of uncertainty.

Source: the introductory definitions and treatment of offspring counts.

Lesson 2

The linear share

A curved relationship can be described closely by a line over the breeding values common in a population. To measure that description, we must specify both the curve and the population.

Separate slope from curvature

Let X be breeding value minus its population mean. Its mean is zero and its variance is G. Near that mean, describe log expected output by a quadratic:

y(X) = c + bX + ½HX2.

The slope b describes the local change in log expected output per unit of breeding value. Curvature H describes how that slope changes. The constant c changes the level of the relationship, but not its variance.

For a normally distributed population, the linear term L = bX and the quadratic term Q = ½HX2 have zero covariance. Their variances therefore add:

Vlin = b2G,   Vquad = ½H2G2,   Ag = Vlin / (Vlin + Vquad).

The linear share Ag is defined when the total variance is positive. A large share identifies an additive channel for this population and fitness relationship. “Additive” here describes the linear relationship across breeding values. It does not identify how individual genes interact.

Calculate the broad and narrow populations

Use b = 1 and H = −1. With G = 1, the linear variance is 12 × 1 = 1. The quadratic variance is ½ × (−1)2 × 12 = 0.5. Their total is 1.5, giving a share of 1/1.5 = 2/3, or 66.7%.

Now reduce G to 0.1 without changing the mean or the curve. The linear variance becomes 0.1. The quadratic variance becomes ½ × 0.12 = 0.005. The share is 0.1/0.105 = 20/21, or 95.2%.

Predict the absolute linear variance

The linear share rose from 66.7% to 95.2%. Did the amount of variation explained by the line increase?

Why the larger share explains less absolute variation

No. Linear variance fell from 1 to 0.1. It forms a larger fraction because quadratic variance fell faster, from 0.5 to 0.005. The ratio alone does not report the amount of variation available.

The fitted line has its own intercept

For this example, the curve is y = X − X2/2. Its tangent at zero is y = X. The best population regression line is y = X − G/2. It lies lower because the average quadratic contribution is negative.

The broad population therefore has fitted line y = X − 0.5, while the narrow population has y = X − 0.05. Both fitted slopes are 1. The line minimises squared prediction errors across the population, weighting each breeding value by how often it occurs. Under the Gaussian quadratic assumptions, Ag is the R2 of this regression with an intercept.

Derive the Gaussian moments and the regression share

Write X = √G U, where U is standard normal. Its density φ satisfies φ′(u) = −uφ(u). Replace uφ(u) by −φ′(u) in the moment integral:

E[Un] = ∫ unφ(u) du = −∫ un−1φ′(u) du.

Integrate by parts over the whole real line. The boundary term −un−1φ(u) vanishes at both limits, because the normal density declines faster than a polynomial grows. The remaining integral is (n − 1)∫ un−2φ(u) du. Hence E[Un] = (n − 1)E[Un−2] for even positive n. Starting from E[1] = 1 gives E[U2] = 1 and E[U4] = 3.

Consequently E[X2] = G and E[X4] = 3G2. Thus

Var(X2) = E[X4] − E[X2]2 = 3G2 − G2 = 2G2.

Multiplication by a constant multiplies variance by its square. Therefore Var(bX) = b2G and Var(HX2/2) = H2(2G2)/4. Symmetry gives E[X3] = 0, so Cov(L,Q) = bH E[X3]/2 = 0.

The regression slope is Cov(X,y)/G = b because Cov(X,Q) = 0. Since E[X] = 0, its intercept is E[y] = c + HG/2. Subtracting the fitted line leaves Q − E[Q]. The residual variance is Vquad, so

R2 = 1 − Vquad/(Vlin + Vquad) = Ag.

When only the quadratic term varies, regression R2 is zero. Correlation with the constant linear term is undefined. If neither term varies, there is no variance to partition and the share is undefined.

Extend the variance calculation to several traits

Let X now be a centred Gaussian vector of breeding values. G is its covariance matrix, b the gradient of log expected output, and H its symmetric curvature matrix. The terms are L = bTX and Q = ½XTHX. Expanding the variance of the linear combination gives

Var(L) = Σi,j bibj Cov(Xi,Xj) = bTGb.

To calculate the quadratic variance, write X = G1/2U, where U has independent standard normal coordinates and G1/2 is the symmetric square root of G. Define S = G1/2HG1/2. This symmetric matrix has orthonormal eigenvectors and real eigenvalues λi. Rotating U into those eigenvectors gives another standard normal vector V, whose coordinates remain independent.

Q = ½Σi λiVi2.

Each squared coordinate has variance 2. Independence removes the covariance terms, giving Var(Q) = ¼Σ λi2 × 2 = ½Σ λi2. The sum of squared eigenvalues is tr(S2), where the trace is the sum of diagonal entries. Multiplication gives S2 = G1/2HGHG1/2. Move the final G1/2 to the front inside the trace; the adjacent square roots multiply to G. Then move that first G to the end. These cyclic rearrangements preserve the trace and give

Vquad = ½ tr(S2) = ½ tr(HGHG).

Every term in Cov(L,Q) contains a centred third Gaussian moment, which is zero. The same variance-share formula follows. These calculations also allow singular G: directions with no variation contribute zero. If the original log-fitness relationship has higher-order terms, the share describes its quadratic approximation, not necessarily the regression fit to the complete relationship.

A two-point population breaks the Gaussian formula

For a separate constructed example, let X equal −1 with probability 2/3 and 2 with probability 1/3. Then E[X] = 0, G = E[X2] = 2, E[X3] = 2 and E[X4] = 6. Its third moment is not zero, and its fourth moment is not 3G2 = 12.

Choose y = X + X2/2. Its two values are −0.5 and 4. The line y = 1 + 1.5X passes through both, so its population R2 is 1. Substituting G = 2, b = 1 and H = 1 into the Gaussian formula would instead give 2/(2 + 2) = 0.5.

For this distribution, Var(L) = 2, Var(Q) = [6 − 22]/4 = 0.5 and Cov(L,Q) = E[X3]/2 = 1. The total variance is therefore 2 + 0.5 + 2 × 1 = 4.5. Dropping the covariance term would be wrong. The derivative at the mean is 1, but the regression slope is 1.5. A curved function can be fitted exactly over two support points. The Gaussian variance-share identity cannot be transferred to this population by replacing G alone.

Sources: the introductory derivation and Gaussian moments and population projection. The two-point calculation above is an additional constructed illustration.

Lesson 3

Changes in population variation

A narrower population can have a larger linear share. Its location matters as well. We can separate these effects by keeping one fitness curve fixed.

Move the population on a fixed curve

Use ℓ(z) = z − z2/2, with expected output WA(z) = exp[ℓ(z)]. Its maximum occurs at z = 1. For population mean μ, define X = Z − μ. Substituting z = μ + X gives the exact expression

ℓ(μ + X) = ℓ(μ) + (1 − μ)X − X2/2.

The local slope is therefore b = 1 − μ and curvature is H = −1. Moving the mean changes the slope experienced by the population even though the curve itself stays fixed.

Predict further narrowing at the peak

If the population at μ = 1 becomes even narrower, will its linear share rise above zero?

Why the share remains zero at the peak

For any positive G, the slope remains zero, so Vlin = 0. Curvature variance falls as G2, but it still accounts for all remaining variation. At exactly G = 0, both variances vanish and the ratio is undefined. Zero linear share does not mean that expected reproduction is equal among the parents in a population with positive variance.

Proportional contraction changes the two components at different rates

For several traits, replace G by sG while keeping the mean, gradient and curvature fixed. Every variance and covariance is multiplied by the same positive number s. The linear variance becomes sVlin, while quadratic variance becomes s2Vquad. Hence

Ag(s) = Vlin / (Vlin + sVquad).

When both components are positive, reducing s raises the share. This calculation describes a comparison at fixed mean and curve. It does not establish that populations must evolve by proportional contraction, or that a changing population keeps the same gradient.

Rotate variation between two traits

Variation also has a direction. Use the fixed two-trait surface ℓ(a) = −0.4‖a − θ‖2, with optimum θ = (0.9375, 0.375) and population mean zero. Its gradient is b = (0.75, 0.30), and H = −0.8I. The squared gradient length is 0.752 + 0.302 = 0.6525.

The population has variance 0.20 along its long axis and 0.005 along its short axis. Rotate those axes relative to the gradient while keeping both variances fixed. The population has the same total breeding-value variance at every angle, but different amounts lie in the direction where log expected reproduction increases.

At 90°, the population still has short-axis variance towards the optimum, so the linear share stays positive. Curvature variance is constant here because the surface has the same curvature in every direction. Other surfaces need not preserve it under rotation.

Calculate the variance components at a rotation angle

Take coordinate u along the gradient and v perpendicular to it. If the long axis is at angle φ to u, its contribution to Var(u) is 0.20 cos2φ. The short axis contributes 0.005 sin2φ. Thus

Vlin = 0.6525[0.20 cos2φ + 0.005 sin2φ].

At 45°, both squared trigonometric terms are 1/2. The variance along the gradient is 0.1025, so Vlin = 0.6525 × 0.1025 = 0.06688125.

Since H = −0.8I, the quadratic variance is ½ × 0.82 tr(G2). Rotation preserves the two covariance eigenvalues, giving Vquad = 0.32(0.202 + 0.0052) = 0.012808. Dividing 0.06688125 by 0.06688125 + 0.012808 gives 0.839276.

This operation changes G relative to a fixed gradient. It differs from rotating the gradient in whitened coordinates while holding its whitened length and the curvature eigenvalues fixed. That latter comparison preserves the variance share because it preserves both variance components.

These comparisons use breeding-value frequencies to describe variation in log expected output. To predict the mean among contributing parents, we must also account for how much each parent contributes. A large linear share has not yet answered that question.

Sources: the fixed-curve population comparison, the two-trait geometry and its numerical record.

Lesson 4

Reproductive weighting and selection

The linear share describes variation at the population's existing frequencies. To calculate which parents contribute to reproduction, we must also account for their expected output. The same fitness relationship enters both calculations, but the weights differ.

Follow the same parents under three weights

Suppose we know the breeding value ai of each of N parents. Parent i has expected reproductive output mi = WA(ai), and we record an offspring count ki. Its breeding value stays the same when we change the weights. What changes is how much that parent contributes to the calculation.

CalculationWeight of parent iMeaning
Existing population1/NEach parent counts once.
Expected reproductive contributionmi / ΣjmjA parent contributes in proportion to its expected output.
Recorded reproductive contributionki / ΣjkjA parent contributes in proportion to its realised offspring count.

For each row, multiply breeding values by the listed weights and add them. The first calculation gives the initial mean. The other two give parental means under expected or realised contributions. Parents with zero offspring remain in the initial population and receive zero realised reproductive weight. If every count is zero, there is no realised reproductive total with which to normalise.

The expected and recorded means can differ because reproduction is variable. For groups with unequal initial frequencies, expected reproductive weights also include those frequencies. A group that is rare but has high expected output may therefore contribute more than its frequency alone would suggest.

These are distributions of parental breeding values. To obtain breeding values in offspring, we must specify inheritance, including how parents are paired and what they transmit. That additional step appears in Lesson 6.

Replace frequency weights with reproductive weights

For a continuous population, let p(a) be the initial breeding-value density. Expected reproduction changes its weight to

psel(a) = p(a)WA(a) / E[WA(A)].

The denominator makes the weights sum to one. An intercept added to log expected output multiplies every parent's expected output by the same positive constant. That factor cancels from the numerator and denominator. It changes total expected reproduction, but not these normalised parental weights.

This explains why correcting the fitted line's intercept does not resolve the selection discrepancy below. Under Gaussian breeding values, the population-fitted line and the tangent have the same slope but different intercepts. Exponentiating either produces the same normalised linear-model weights.

A 97% linear share and a 50% overestimate

Use one trait, with the initial mean set to zero and variance G = 1. Let the log conditional mean be exactly quadratic:

ℓA(a) = ℓ0 + 2a − a2/4.

Thus b = 2 and H = −0.5. The variance due to the linear term is b2G = 4. The centred quadratic term contributes H2G2/2 = 0.125. Their linear share is 4/4.125 = 32/33, approximately 96.97%.

Now weight the original normal population by expected reproduction. With stabilising curvature γ = −H, completing the square gives selected-parent variance G/(1 + γG) and mean shift Gb/(1 + γG). Ignoring curvature gives the shift Gb.

QuantitySubstitutionResult
Selected-parent variance1/(1 + 0.5 × 1)2/3
Exact mean shift1 × 2/(1 + 0.5 × 1)4/3
Shift when curvature is omitted1 × 22
Error relative to the exact shift(2 − 4/3)/(4/3)1/2 = 50%

The calculation is internally consistent. The linear share is the fraction of log-scale variance captured by the linear term under initial population frequencies. The shift depends on exponentiated log expected output under reproductive weighting. A small relative residual variance in the first calculation does not force a small error in the second.

Increase the slope while keeping curvature and variance fixed

Before changing the controls, predict what happens when b rises from 2 to 10. The linear share rises. Must the proportional error in the selected-parent shift fall?

Compare the two calculations

The share becomes 100/100.125, about 99.88%. The linear-model shift is 10 and the exact shift is 10/1.5. Their relative discrepancy remains 50%, because it depends on γG = 0.5. Here a steeper slope increases both shifts without improving their proportional agreement.

Check the assumptions before using the exact shift

The preceding formulas require Gaussian breeding values and an exactly quadratic log conditional mean over the occupied support. A Taylor expansion near the mean does not by itself justify treating that quadratic as the complete reproductive relationship. Reproductive weighting may make parts of the distribution influential where the local approximation is less accurate.

The normalising integral must also be finite. For one trait the condition is 1 + γG > 0. Stabilising curvature satisfies it. Curvature of the opposite sign can still be integrable, but not when expected output increases too rapidly relative to the Gaussian decline in frequency. At the boundary, a zero gradient does not rescue the divergent integral.

Derive the selected Gaussian distribution by completing the square

Centre the breeding-value vector: x = a − μ. Its covariance G is positive definite. Write the exact log mean as ℓ0 + bTx − xTΓx/2, where Γ = −H is symmetric.

The Gaussian density contributes −xTG−1x/2 to the exponent. Multiplying by expected output adds their exponents:

−½xTJx + bTx,   J = G−1 + Γ.

Every eigenvalue of J must be positive. Along an eigenvector with eigenvalue λ, the integral contains exp(−λy2/2 + dy). A positive λ gives a finite Gaussian integral. A negative λ gives growth in the tails; at zero the remaining exponential or constant is also nonintegrable.

Set C = J−1 and m = Cb. Since Jm = b, expanding the centred quadratic gives

(x − m)TJ(x − m) = xTJx − 2bTx + bTCb.

Rearrange and integrate the centred Gaussian. Mean output is

exp(ℓ0) det(G)−1/2 det(J)−1/2 exp(½bTCb).

Dividing by this value leaves a Gaussian with covariance C and centred mean Cb. Therefore Gsel = (G−1 + Γ)−1 and Δfull = Gselb. Setting Γ to zero gives Δlin = Gb. No simultaneous diagonalisation of covariance and curvature was assumed.

Measure the multivariate shift error in covariance units

Several traits can differ in units and covariance, and the two shift vectors need not point in the same direction. Define the displacement length as ‖v‖G−1 = √(vTG−1v). This length accounts for covariance and stays unchanged under an invertible change of trait coordinates applied consistently.

Let T = G1/2ΓG1/2 and c = G1/2b. In covariance-standardised coordinates, the exact shift is y = (I + T)−1c. The linear shift is c. Since c = (I + T)y, their difference is Ty.

‖Δlin − Δfull‖G−1 / ‖Δfull‖G−1 = ‖Ty‖/‖y‖ ≤ ‖T‖2 ≤ √(2Vquad).

The first bound is the largest absolute eigenvalue of T. It cannot exceed the square root of the sum of squared eigenvalues, equal here to √(2Vquad). This uses absolute curvature variance, whereas the linear share is a ratio. The bound requires a finite normaliser and a nonzero shift. When the gradient vanishes, both shifts are zero and their relative error is undefined. With singular covariance, restrict the calculation to directions containing variation. A percentage difference between ordinary vector lengths is a different measure.

Check the one-trait shift by numerical integration in Python

This quadrature weights the population directly, independently of the square-completion formula.

import numpy as np
from numpy.polynomial.hermite import hermgauss

# Exact Gaussian population and quadratic log expected output.
b, H, G = 2.0, -0.5, 1.0
nodes, raw_weights = hermgauss(80)
x = np.sqrt(2 * G) * nodes
p = raw_weights / np.sqrt(np.pi)

# The omitted intercept cancels from normalised weights.
log_output = b * x + 0.5 * H * x**2
weights = p * np.exp(log_output - log_output.max())
weights /= weights.sum()
selected_mean = weights @ x
selected_variance = weights @ (x - selected_mean)**2

linear_variance = b**2 * G
curved_variance = 0.5 * H**2 * G**2
share = linear_variance / (linear_variance + curved_variance)
linear_shift = G * b
relative_error = abs(linear_shift - selected_mean) / abs(selected_mean)
print(share, selected_mean, selected_variance, relative_error)
# Approximately: 0.969697, 1.333333, 0.666667, 0.5

Lesson 5

Estimating the share from measurements

The previous lessons specify breeding values and a fitness relationship. In an experiment, both have to be estimated. Repeated trait measurements help describe uncertainty in breeding values; reproductive counts help estimate the fitness relationship.

The simulated plant population

The repository's worked example contains 800 unrelated plant genotypes. Each has three height measurements and one count of surviving offspring. The measurements are simulated under a wholly additive trait model, with independent environmental deviations and a separate reproductive assay. The true breeding values are retained in a separate file for checking the analysis. The estimator never receives them.

This is a deliberately restricted teaching model. Repeated measurements of a clone do not, on their own, distinguish additive genetic effects from dominance, epistasis or permanent environmental effects. Applying the approach to breeding data would require a design and genetic model that identify the breeding-value component. A pedigree or genomic relationship model would be an extension of this example.

These parameters differ from the introductory curve. They belong to this recorded estimation example.

Retain uncertainty in each breeding value

A genotype's three heights give a noisy estimate of its breeding value. The analysis first estimates variation within genotypes, then subtracts the environmental contribution from the variance of genotype means. Under the stated normal model, each set of measurements gives a normal conditional distribution for the centred breeding value.

The conditional mean is pulled towards the population mean by an amount determined by measurement reliability. Its conditional variance records uncertainty that remains after the repeated measurements. The fitness fit averages the probability of the observed offspring count over that distribution. Treating the conditional mean as an exact observation would discard this uncertainty.

Calculate genetic variance and the conditional breeding-value distribution

Let Yij = μ + Xi + eij, where Xi has mean zero and variance G, and the independent measurement errors have variance E. With m measurements per genotype, the mean error has variance E/m. Therefore

Var(Ȳi) = G + E/m.

The balanced analysis estimates E from within-genotype differences:

Ê = Σi,j(Yij − Ȳi)² / [n(m − 1)].

It then estimates G as the sample variance of genotype means minus Ê/m. The sample variance uses n − 1 in its denominator. The estimated overall mean μ̂ is the average of the genotype means. In the saved dataset, Ĝ = 1.002148809, Ê = 0.979881353 and μ̂ = −0.019910277 cm.

Under normality, conditional expectation gives the reliability

r = Ĝ / (Ĝ + Ê/m) = 0.754189468.

The centred conditional mean and variance for genotype i are

Mi = r(Ȳi − μ̂),   v = Ĝ(1 − r).

The same result follows by multiplying the normal prior density for X by the normal likelihood for the mean height and completing the square. Their precisions add: 1/v = 1/Ĝ + m/Ê. Multiplying v by m(Ȳi − μ̂)/Ê gives Mi.

If the measurements do not resolve a positive Ĝ, the repository stops the calibration. It does not silently substitute a small positive variance and continue.

Integrate the offspring-count likelihood over breeding-value uncertainty

For a centred breeding value x, the primary model uses a Poisson count with mean λ(x) = exp(α + bx + Hx²/2). For an observed integer count k, the conditional probability is exp[−λ(x)]λ(x)k/k!. A zero count contributes exp[−λ(x)], so it supplies information about the fitness relationship.

The likelihood contribution of genotype i averages this count probability over its height-conditioned normal distribution:

Li(α,b,H) = ∫ Poisson[Wi; λ(x)] Normal(x; Mi,v) dx.

The fit maximises the sum of ln Li across all genotypes. This is a two-stage procedure: the measurement model is calibrated first, then its distributions are used in the fitness likelihood. It is not a joint animal-model or genomic analysis.

The repository evaluates the integral by normal quadrature and increases the number of points to check stability. The saved primary fit used 41 points and was checked at 81. The per-record probability displayed above uses 81 points from the same standard-normal quadrature rule. Changing the selected record only changes which likelihood contribution is inspected.

After fitting, the same population formula gives V̂lin = b̂²Ĝ and V̂quad = Ĥ²Ĝ²/2. Estimating the coefficients does not change what the resulting share describes.

The saved estimate and its uncertainty

The fitted slope is approximately 0.6826 and curvature is −0.7698. Together with Ĝ = 1.0021, they give a linear variance of 0.4669 and a curvature variance of 0.2976. The estimated share is 0.610744.

To assess sampling uncertainty under the fitted model, the analysis generated 500 new simulated datasets. Each repeat regenerated both height measurements and offspring counts, recalibrated the measurement model, and refitted the fitness relationship. The 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of those refits give a model-conditional 95% interval of 0.533024–0.685713. The display below shows those saved refits.

The interval reflects sampling variation under the chosen measurement and quadratic Poisson models. It does not account for an incorrect genetic model, unmodelled relatedness, an unsuitable count distribution or a fitness curve that the quadratic fails to represent.

A closer estimate in one dataset can still come from an unsuitable method

The saved comparison that treats predicted breeding values as exact gives a share of 0.673984. That happens to lie closer to the generating value 2/3 than 0.610744 does. It would be misleading to describe this single example as showing that accounting for uncertainty always moves an estimate closer to the truth.

Both slope and curvature are refitted in the comparison, and the variance of the shrunken predictions replaces G. The direction of the resulting discrepancy is not fixed. Comparing methods requires repeated datasets or appropriate validation, with attention to component estimates and interval behaviour as well as the ratio.

Interpret a narrow interval

Suppose a much larger dataset produced a narrow interval around the fitted quadratic share. Would that establish that the quadratic describes the full fitness relationship accurately?

Check the fitted relationship as well as the interval

No. The interval can be narrow because the parameters of the chosen model are estimated precisely. The chosen model may still omit features of the underlying relationship. The comparison below keeps the local slope and curvature unchanged while adding a fourth-order term.

Local curvature can miss a broader change in the curve

Consider a separate, fully specified mathematical comparison: ℓ(x) = ln(4) + 0.7x − 0.35x² − κx⁴, with X normally distributed and G = 1. The fourth-order term has zero first and second derivatives at the mean. The local quadratic share therefore remains 2/3 as κ changes.

The best linear description of the complete log mean can nevertheless become much poorer. At κ = 0.08 its population R² is approximately 0.242406. This calculation concerns the specified generating curve. It is not a new fit to the saved 800-genotype dataset.

Calculate the full-curve R² with a fourth-order term

Write L = bX and Q = HX²/2 − κX⁴. A centred Gaussian distribution is symmetric, so Cov(L,Q) = 0: all the cross-products have odd total powers. The best linear slope remains b.

The required Gaussian moments are E[X²] = G, E[X⁴] = 3G², E[X⁶] = 15G³ and E[X⁸] = 105G⁴. Hence Var(X²) = 2G², Var(X⁴) = 96G⁴ and Cov(X²,X⁴) = 12G³. Expanding the variance of Q gives

Var(Q) = H²G²/2 − 12HκG³ + 96κ²G⁴.

Therefore the full-curve R² is b²G/[b²G + H²G²/2 − 12HκG³ + 96κ²G⁴]. Substituting b = 0.7, H = −0.7, G = 1 and κ = 0.08 gives linear variance 0.49 and even-term variance 1.5314. The linear fraction of their sum is 0.49/2.0214 ≈ 0.242406.

The calculation shows why the scope of the approximation has to accompany the share. An exact decomposition of the quadratic description does not establish that the quadratic describes the full relationship accurately.

Lesson 6

Inheritance and changing genetic variation

Reproductive weighting tells us which parental values contribute. Variation in the next generation also depends on what reproduction does to associations among alleles. The model below specifies that step, allowing us to ask whether genetic variation persists under repeated stabilising selection.

Separate genic covariance from allele associations

Assume an additive genetic architecture with fixed allelic effect vectors αj. If the designated allele has frequency pj, random association of allele copies within and between loci gives the genic covariance

B = 2 Σj pj(1 − pj)αjαjT.

The factor 2p(1 − p) is the diploid allele-count variance under random association. An allele affecting several traits contributes covariance even when different loci are independent. Allele associations change covariance from this baseline: write G = B + D. Unlike a covariance matrix, D can reduce variance in a trait combination.

Fixed effects do not imply fixed genic variance. With one locus and effect 1, changing its frequency from 0.5 to 0.8 changes 2p(1 − p) from 0.50 to 0.32. The model holds B fixed as an additional assumption, excluding arbitrary allele-frequency change.

Specify the inheritance step

In each generation, assume Gaussian breeding values with positive-definite covariance and an exact quadratic log conditional mean with fixed stabilising curvature Γ = −H ≽ 0. Selection gives Gtsel = (Gt−1 + Γ)−1. For the next covariance, retain a fraction ρ of the selected departure from the genic baseline:

Dt+1 = ρ(Gtsel − B),
Gt+1 = (1 − ρ)B + ρ(Gt−1 + Γ)−1.

Here B is positive definite and 0 ≤ ρ < 1. The retained departure includes associations already present and their change during selection. If those associations reduce variation, reducing their magnitude restores some variation towards B. The baseline term does not introduce new alleles or represent mutation.

The scalar ρ is not a universal recombination fraction, selfing rate or inbreeding coefficient. Different locus pairs and mating systems need not act through one multiplier. Gaussian distributions in successive generations are also assumed: a covariance rule alone does not establish their shape.

Obtain half retention from a specified midparent and segregation model

Let two parents be independent draws from the same reproductively weighted distribution. Write an offspring breeding value as Ao = (A1 + A2)/2 + M, with E[M | parents] = 0. The zero conditional mean makes the segregation deviation uncorrelated with the midparent value.

If the mean conditional segregation covariance is B/2, the law of total covariance gives

Cov(Ao) = ¼(Gsel + Gsel) + ½B = ½Gsel + ½B.

This supplies the benchmark ρ = 1/2 under those assumptions. Independence of parents and the stipulated segregation covariance are required; the general retention rule is a separate model assumption.

Calculate successive variances for one trait

Use lowercase g for a single trait's variance, B for its fixed genic variance, and γ for stabilising curvature. The update becomes

gt+1 = (1 − ρ)B + ρgt/(1 + γgt).

In the mathematics guide, B = 1, γ = 0.5, ρ = 0.5 and g0 = 1. The first selected variance is 1/1.5 = 2/3. Reproduction gives g1 = 1/2 + (1/2)(2/3) = 5/6. The next two values are 27/34 and 149/190, approximately 0.7941 and 0.7842. They approach the fixed point (√17 − 1)/4, approximately 0.7808.

The fixed point is a variance left unchanged by the update. It is unique for this scalar model, and every nonnegative starting variance converges to it. A start above equilibrium decreases; a start below it increases. Reduced variance among contributing parents therefore need not mean reduced variance between generations.

Start below the covariance equilibrium

Set B = 1, γ = 0.3 and ρ = 0.9, as in the paper's trajectory, but start at g = 0.1. Will the next generation have less variance because stabilising selection reduces the selected-parent variance?

Calculate selection and reproduction separately

Selection reduces variance to 0.1/1.03, approximately 0.09709. The next variance is 0.1 + 0.9(0.1/1.03) = 0.18738. Reproduction more than offsets this episode's reduction because the starting variance is below the equilibrium of 0.47235. The two steps answer different biological questions.

Solve the scalar equilibrium and prove convergence

Set g* = (1 − ρ)B + ρg*/(1 + γg*). Multiplication by the positive denominator and collection of terms gives

γ(g*)2 + (1 − ρ)(1 − γB)g* − (1 − ρ)B = 0.

For γ > 0 the roots have opposite signs. Define c = (1 − ρ)(1 − γB) and d = (1 − ρ)B. The positive root is

g* = [−c + √(c2 + 4γd)]/(2γ).

For c ≥ 0, the equivalent form 2d/[√(c2 + 4γd) + c] avoids subtracting similar numbers. With zero curvature, solve the original equation directly to obtain g* = B.

Existence of a root does not establish convergence. For two variances g, h ≥ 0, subtract their updates:

F(g) − F(h) = ρ(g − h)/[(1 + γg)(1 + γh)].

The denominator is at least one. Each update therefore reduces distance by at most the factor ρ < 1. Comparing an iterate with the fixed point repeatedly gives |gt − g*| ≤ ρt|g0 − g*|, which tends to zero. Two different fixed points would contradict the same inequality. The exact difference preserves the sign of the deviation, so a trajectory does not cross equilibrium.

Establish matrix existence and bounds without claiming general convergence

For matrices, C ≼ D means vTCv ≤ vTDv for every vector v. This compares variance in every trait combination, rather than individual covariance entries.

With T = G1/2ΓG1/2 ≽ 0, selected covariance is G1/2(I + T)−1G1/2. Its middle factor has eigenvalues between zero and one, hence 0 ≺ Gsel ≼ G.

If (1 − ρ)B ≼ G ≼ B, its update remains within those bounds. This matrix interval is nonempty, compact and convex; the update is continuous there. Brouwer's theorem therefore supplies at least one positive-definite fixed point. Any positive fixed point satisfies

(1 − ρ)B ≼ G* ≼ B.

The lower bound follows from the nonnegative selected term. For the upper bound, use G* ≼ (1 − ρ)B + ρG*, subtract the last term and divide by 1 − ρ. This proves existence and bounds, without establishing matrix uniqueness, convergence or immediate entry into this interval from every starting matrix.

A covariance trajectory does not specify the gradient

The paper's four-trait example uses Gt = gtI, g0 = 4, B = 1, γ = 0.3 and ρ = 0.9. Its fixed point is 0.4723532349. It separately holds the gradient length at 1 through generation 40, then sets it to exp[−0.06(t − 40)]. The share rises from 0.581395 to 0.921639 while the gradient is maintained, then falls as the supplied gradient declines.

The covariance recursion did not predict that gradient history. In n isotropic trait directions, the share is ‖bt‖2/[‖bt‖2 + nγ2gt/2]. Its interpretation therefore requires both covariance and gradient. On a fixed quadratic surface, movement of the mean changes the gradient through bt = d − Γμt. Predicting that movement needs a mean-transmission model.

Examine complete retention at ρ = 1

The baseline term now vanishes. For a positive starting variance g0 > 0, 1/gt+1 = 1/gt + γ, giving gt = 1/(1/g0 + tγ). Positive curvature drives variance towards zero; zero curvature leaves it unchanged. A starting variance of zero remains zero under the original scalar update.

For positive-definite initial covariance, Gt = (G0−1 + tΓ)−1. Positive-definite curvature removes all covariance in the limit. With uncurved directions, retained variation depends on their initial associations with curved directions. For initial covariance [[1, 0.4], [0.4, 2]] and curvature diag(0.5, 0), the second trait retains 2 − 0.42/1 = 1.84, its conditional residual variance, rather than its original marginal variance of 2.

Reproduce the guide's scalar trajectory in Python
import math

# These are the guide's parameters, not the four-trait figure's.
B, gamma, rho, g = 1.0, 0.5, 0.5, 1.0
c = (1 - rho) * (1 - gamma * B)
d = (1 - rho) * B
root = math.sqrt(c*c + 4*gamma*d)
fixed_point = 2*d / (root + c) if c >= 0 else (root - c)/(2*gamma)

for generation in range(11):
    print(generation, g, abs(g - fixed_point))
    selected_variance = g / (1 + gamma*g)
    # Reproduction acts on the departure from the fixed baseline.
    g = B + rho*(selected_variance - B)
print("Fixed point:", fixed_point)
