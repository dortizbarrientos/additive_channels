# Teaching notes for the slides

## 1. How much fitness variation can a straight line describe?

Start with variation in reproductive output. Ask students what they would need to know about each plant and about the population. Refer to the teaching notes, pages 1–2.

## 2. Average offspring counts before taking logs

A breeding value sums average allelic effects on a trait in a specified population. The individual outcome can vary at the same breeding value. The plot shows sample means, not the full population curve.

## 3. Which breeding values occur most often?

The tangent at zero is y=x. The population regression line has slope 1 and intercept −G/2. The intercept changes because the mean quadratic contribution changes.

## 4. Slope contributes one factor of G; curvature contributes two

Ask for the variance of X². E[X⁴] − E[X²]² equals 3G² − G² = 2G² for a normal distribution. Symmetry alone does not give that fourth moment.

## 5. A larger share can accompany less linear variance

State the target: population regression of the quadratic log conditional mean on breeding value, with an intercept. At zero slope and positive quadratic variance the regression R² is zero, but correlation with a constant is undefined.

## 6. Does a 97% fit bound the error in selection?

The share is 32/33, about 97%. The linear fit minimises squared error in log expected output. Selection weights by expected reproductive output on its original scale.

## 7. The exact shift is 4/3; ignoring curvature gives 2

The denominator is the exact shift. Neither calculation predicts the offspring mean without transmission assumptions. This is a good stopping point for a 35-minute class.
