# Teaching notes for the slides

## 1. How much fitness variation can a straight line describe?

Start with variation in reproductive output. Ask students what they would need to know about each plant and about the population. Refer to the teaching notes, pages 1–2.

## 2. Zero offspring counts still contribute to the mean

A breeding value sums average allelic effects on a trait in a specified population. The individual outcome can vary at the same breeding value. The plot shows sample means, not the full population curve.

## 3. Which breeding values occur most often?

Ask students to predict the change before showing the calculation. The tangent at zero is y=x. The population regression line has slope 1 and intercept −G/2. The intercept changes from −0.5 to −0.05 because the mean quadratic contribution changes. These are the first two populations in the one-trait visual walkthrough.

## 4. Why does the curved contribution decline faster?

Ask for the variance of X². E[X⁴] − E[X²]² equals 3G² − G² = 2G² for a normal distribution. Symmetry alone does not give that fourth moment. At fixed slope and curvature, multiplying G by 0.1 multiplies the linear variance by 0.1 and the quadratic variance by 0.01.

## 5. A larger share can accompany less linear variance

The component pairs are (1, 0.5) and (0.1, 0.005). Their linear shares are 66.7% and 95.2%. Ask students to explain why the share rises while both components fall. State the target: population regression of the quadratic log conditional mean on breeding value, with an intercept. At a peak the slope is zero and the quadratic variance remains positive, so regression R² is zero. Narrowing the distribution does not change that zero share.

## 6. Does a 97% fit bound the error in selection?

The share is 32/33, about 97%. Explain that this is a second fitness curve, chosen to compare fit and selection. Before showing the next slide, ask which weights each calculation uses. The linear fit minimises squared error in log expected output under population frequencies. Selection additionally weights by expected reproductive output on its original scale.

## 7. Ignoring curvature overestimates the shift by 50%

The denominator is the exact shift. Neither calculation predicts the offspring mean without transmission assumptions. This is a good stopping point for a 35-minute class. Continue with the parental-weighting explorer to follow individual parents under the two weighting rules.
